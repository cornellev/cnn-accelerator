Why 16 multiplies beat 36

Same 4×4 pixel tile, same 3×3 kernel, same four outputs. Three ways to get there, and the only thing that really differs is how many multiplications each one needs. Every number on this page is computed live from the tile and kernel you set below.

1 · The baseline

Direct convolution repeats itself

Each output slides the 3×3 kernel over a 3×3 window and does 9 multiplies. Four outputs means 36. But the four windows overlap heavily. There are only 16 distinct pixels and 9 distinct weights, and the centre four pixels get multiplied in all four windows. That redundancy is the opening Winograd exploits.

2 · The trick, in one dimension

Add first, multiply once, let the extras cancel

Shrink the problem to a row: 4 pixels, 3 taps, 2 outputs. That is F(2,3). Direct needs 6 products. Winograd builds 4 products where each one multiplies a sum of pixels by a sum of weights. Each product contains some terms you want and some you don't. The unwanted terms come in ± pairs across different products, so they cancel when you add the products together.

The weight sums like (g₀+g₁+g₂)/2 depend only on the kernel. In a CNN the kernel is fixed after training, so they are computed once and stored. That is the u_weights input in your RTL. The pixel sums like d₁+d₂ are plain adders. Only the 4 marked products cost a multiplier.

Where those weight sums come from: evaluating a polynomial

Read the kernel as a polynomial g(x) = g₀ + g₁x + g₂x². Convolution is polynomial multiplication, and the standard trick for multiplying polynomials fast is: evaluate both at a few points, multiply the values pointwise, then interpolate back. Each point costs exactly one multiplication. Winograd and FFT are this same recipe with different points.

This is the whole story. FFT evaluates at the 4th roots of unity 1, −i, −1, i. Two of those are complex, so their products are complex multiplies (3–4 real multiplies each). Winograd keeps the two real points 1 and −1, and swaps the complex pair for 0 and ∞. g(0)=g₀ and g(∞) is just the leading coefficient g₂. Every value stays a small real integer, so each point is one cheap real multiply, and the transforms are only 0, ±1 (adds and subtracts).

4 · The same tile through an FFT

FFT does the same thing at complex points

A 4×4 2D DFT of the tile, a 4×4 DFT of the zero-padded kernel, one pointwise product, one inverse DFT. A 4-point DFT only uses ±1 and ±i, so at this size the transforms are also just adds. The cost is in the middle: the 16 values are complex. Because the input is real, half of them mirror the other half as conjugates and can be skipped. That leaves 4 real products and 6 complex ones. At 3 real multiplies per complex product, that is 4 + 18 = 22.

Circular wrap. FFT computes a circular correlation, so the kernel wraps around the tile edges. Only the top-left 2×2 never wraps, and it matches the direct result exactly. The other 12 values are thrown away. Bigger FFTs keep a much larger fraction of their outputs, which is where FFT starts to win (section 6).

5 · What it costs on your FPGA

Multipliers are the scarce resource

On an FPGA, each multiply maps to a DSP slice and each add maps to cheap LUT/carry-chain logic. A DSP48 multiplies 25×18 bits in one block, so a 9×8 product and a 12×11 product cost the same: one DSP. Saving multiplies is a direct saving in the resource you run out of first. Adds are close to free by comparison.

Why it's faster

For the same 16 DSPs, direct produces 16/9 ≈ 1.8 outputs per cycle and Winograd produces 4. That is the 2.25× gain. You pay with 24 extra adders and wider multipliers. On an FPGA that's a good trade because the DSP count is fixed and the adders are cheap. In a real layer it gets better: V for a tile is computed once and reused for every output channel, so the input-transform adds are spread over many kernels.

Why not always bigger tiles?

F(4×4,3×3) needs 36 multiplies for 16 outputs, which is 4× fewer than direct. It has to evaluate at extra points like ±2 and ±½. The transform constants grow, intermediate bit widths grow, and in floating point the error blows up. That's why F(2×2,3×3) and F(4×4,3×3) are where most designs stop. FFT doesn't have this problem, since roots of unity all have size 1. The price is complex arithmetic.

6 · When FFT wins

Multiplies per output as the kernel grows

Direct cost grows as k². Winograd grows slower but is stuck with small tiles. FFT cost barely depends on k: a large transform does a fixed amount of work per frequency point, whatever the kernel size. The catch is the transforms themselves. On a single channel they cost more multiplies than they save. In a deep layer with many channels, each transformed input is reused across all output channels, so the transform cost almost disappears.

FFT transforms

Log scale. FFT uses the best tile size N ∈ {8, 16, 32, 64} with overlap-save, a radix-2 real-input estimate, and 3 real multiplies per complex product. Kernel transforms are precomputed for every method. These are multiply counts only. Memory traffic, precision and control logic aren't counted.
